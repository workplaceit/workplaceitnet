Moving or Building Out an Office in Denver? Your Network Cabling Checklist

November 6, 2026 • 8 min read • By Workplace IT

Network cabling is the one part of an office move or build-out that's painful to fix after the walls close and your team moves in. Use this checklist to get it right the first time.

Furniture can be rearranged and switches can be swapped, but cabling lives inside walls and ceilings. Miss a drop, pick the wrong cable, or skip documentation and you'll be paying to open things back up later, usually while your team is trying to work around it.

Whether you're moving into a new space, building out a new floor, or finally fixing the closet nobody wants to open, here's what to plan.

1. Bring Cabling In Early

The best time to plan cabling is when you have a floor plan, before construction starts. That gives your cabling contractor time to review outlet locations, plan pathways and conduit during rough-in, and coordinate with the general contractor and electrician instead of working around them. Waiting until the drywall is up limits your options and usually costs more.

2. Count Every Drop, Not Just the Desks

Desks are the obvious part. The drops that get forgotten are the devices that don't sit on a desk. Walk the floor plan and count:

  • Workstations and docking stations
  • Wireless access points, usually in the ceiling
  • VoIP phones
  • IP security cameras, including exterior locations
  • Access control readers and intercoms
  • Printers, copiers, and label printers
  • Conference room displays and AV equipment
  • Any specialty equipment on the shop or warehouse floor

Then add spare drops in the places most likely to change, such as open office areas and conference rooms. A spare pulled during construction costs far less than one added after.

3. Choose Cat6 or Cat6A

Cat6 supports 10 Gigabit Ethernet up to 55 meters and works for most office environments. Cat6A supports 10GbE up to the full 100 meters and offers better crosstalk performance. For new builds, data centers, or anywhere you want full-distance 10G capability, we recommend Cat6A where the budget allows. The incremental cost is small compared with re-cabling later.

For connections between floors or buildings, or anywhere copper can't reach, fiber is the answer. We install OM3/OM4 multimode and OS2 single-mode fiber, with fusion splicing available.

4. Plan the Network Room (MDF/IDF)

Every cable run ends somewhere, and that room deserves as much planning as the outlets. Consider:

  • Location: copper runs have distance limits, so larger spaces may need more than one closet (IDF) connected by fiber
  • Rack layout: patch panels, switches, firewall, and room to grow
  • Power: dedicated circuits and a UPS to ride through short outages
  • Cooling and access: equipment needs airflow, and the room needs to be secured
  • Internet service: where your provider's service enters the building and how it reaches the rack

5. Use the Right Cable for the Space

Cable run through air-handling spaces, such as many drop ceilings, typically needs to be plenum-rated. Outdoor and underground runs need outdoor-rated or direct-burial cable, or conduit. Your cabling contractor should confirm the right rating for each pathway with the general contractor and the building's requirements.

6. Coordinate With the Other Trades

Cabling crews share the ceiling with electricians, HVAC, and AV installers. Good coordination with the general contractor and electricians keeps pathways clear, avoids rework, and keeps the schedule on track. Ask your cabling contractor how they coordinate with the rest of the project.

7. Schedule Around Your Business

For an existing space, ask about nights, weekends, or phased installs to minimize disruption. As a rough guide, a small office of 10 to 20 drops typically takes about a day, and a 50 to 100 drop floor usually takes two to three days. Larger projects with rack build-outs or underground runs are scoped individually.

8. Require Testing and Documentation

This is the step that separates a professional install from a future headache. Before you sign off, you should receive:

  • Certification test results for every copper run, using a Fluke DSX or equivalent analyzer
  • OTDR test results for every fiber run
  • Labeling on every jack and patch panel port, following the TIA-606 standard
  • As-built documentation: port map, rack diagram, and labeled photos

With that package, your team or any future IT provider knows exactly what every cable does.

9. Clean Up the Old Cable

Moving out of a space or upgrading an existing one? Abandoned cable left in ceilings creates clutter, makes future work harder, and can be a code issue. Plan for removal of dead cable as part of the project.

Questions to Ask a Cabling Contractor

  1. Will you do a site survey before quoting?
  2. Is the quote itemized by drop count, materials, and labor?
  3. Is every run certified with test reports delivered at the end?
  4. Do you label to TIA-606 and provide as-built documentation?
  5. Can you work nights or weekends if needed?
  6. Do you stand behind your workmanship?

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Cat6 and Cat6A?

Cat6 supports 10GbE up to 55 meters and suits most office environments. Cat6A supports 10GbE up to 100 meters with better crosstalk performance, and is recommended for new builds and anywhere you need full-distance 10G capability.

How long does office network cabling take?

A small office of 10 to 20 drops typically takes about a day. A 50 to 100 drop floor usually takes two to three days. Larger projects with server room build-outs or underground runs are scoped individually.

Can you cable an existing office without tearing up the walls?

In most cases, yes. Flexible drill bits, cable fishing tools, and surface-mount raceways make it possible to add drops to finished spaces. A site survey identifies the best pathways first.
