5 Practical AI Automation Examples for Denver Businesses (and How to Keep Them Secure)

November 6, 2026 • 7 min read • By Workplace IT

Most businesses don't need more AI hype. They need the repetitive work off their team's plate. Here are five automations we build for Denver businesses, what each one replaces, and the guardrails that keep them from becoming a security problem.

Ask a business owner where their team loses time and the answer is rarely "we need a chatbot." It's retyping the same data into two systems. It's the new hire who waits two days for accounts. It's the laptop that gets set up a little differently every time. It's the same five questions landing in IT's inbox every week.

Those are the tasks where AI and automation pay off first. Below are five real examples, starting with one we recently built for a client.

How to Spot a Task Worth Automating

Before the examples, a quick filter. A task is a strong automation candidate when it is:

  • Repetitive: the same steps, done the same way, over and over
  • Rule-based: a person could write the steps down as a checklist
  • Copy-and-paste heavy: data is retyped from one application into another
  • Waiting on someone: work stalls until IT or one specific employee gets to it
  • Error-prone: missed steps or typos cause rework later

If a task checks two or three of those boxes, it's worth a conversation.

1. Site Survey to Proposal in One Click

One of our clients completes project surveys in System Surveyor. After every survey, someone spent hours re-entering each item and labor line into their quoting software to build a proposal. Same data, typed twice, with every chance for a transcription error.

We connected the two applications. Now the team finishes the survey exactly the way they always have, pushes one button, and every item and labor line flows into the quoting software and creates the proposal. Hours of work now take minutes, and nobody retypes anything.

The pattern to look for: any time your team exports, re-enters, or copies data from one system into another, there's a good chance the two systems can be connected instead.

2. User Onboarding Automation

Onboarding a new employee usually means creating accounts, assigning licenses, setting permissions, and adding the person to the right groups and distribution lists. Done by hand, steps get missed, and the new hire's first day turns into a day of waiting.

Automated onboarding creates the accounts, licenses, permissions, and group memberships the same way every time, based on the role. It's faster, it's consistent, and it's easier to audit, which matters if you have compliance requirements.

3. Computer Deployment Automation

When every PC is set up by hand, every PC ends up a little different: different settings, different software versions, a security control missed here and there. Automated deployment means new devices arrive configured, secured, and ready to use, the same way every time, with far less hands-on time from IT.

4. Connecting Applications That Don't Talk to Each Other

Example one is a specific case of a broader problem: most businesses run on several applications that were never designed to share data. CRM, quoting, accounting, ticketing, scheduling. When they don't talk, people become the integration layer.

An integration moves the data between systems automatically. The result is less retyping, fewer errors, and a single source of truth instead of three slightly different versions of the same customer record.

5. Custom AI Agents for Internal Answers and Troubleshooting

This is where AI itself does the heavy lifting. A custom AI agent connects to the internal information sources you approve, such as documentation, procedures, and knowledge bases, and gives employees fast answers instead of making them search or open a ticket. Agents can also automate common troubleshooting steps.

The key word is approved. An agent should only reach the sources you deliberately give it, which brings us to security.

Not Everything Needs AI

Some of the best automations use AI to build or design the workflow and then step out of the way. Once the process is defined, a plain integration or script runs it without any AI touching your data. Where that's possible, we take AI out of the loop entirely. Less access means less risk.

Security-First Automation: The Guardrails

Automation should never create a new way for data to leak. Every automation we build is designed around what it actually needs to touch, and nothing more:

  • Least-privilege access: AI and automations get only the access required for the specific task
  • Approved data sources: agents are limited to the information you approve
  • AI out of the way: where possible, AI is removed once the automation is built
  • Written AI policy: clear rules for how your team uses AI tools
  • User training: employees learn what is safe to share with AI and what isn't
  • Data Loss Prevention (DLP): controls that alert on or block sensitive data before it reaches an AI tool

If you're a defense contractor, this matters even more. AI use has to be designed around what data it can and can't touch so that automation doesn't create CMMC compliance problems. For more on the governance side, see our guide to AI security and data loss prevention.

How to Get Started

You don't need an AI strategy document to start. Pick one task your team hates and write down four things:

  1. What the task is, step by step
  2. How often it happens and how long it takes
  3. Which applications are involved
  4. Who does it, and who waits on it

That's enough for us to tell you whether it can be automated, what it would take, and how to do it securely.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between AI and automation?

Automation follows defined steps, like moving data between two systems or creating a user account. AI adds judgment, such as reading a question and finding the right answer in your documentation. Many of the best results use plain automation, with AI only where it adds value.

Is it safe to give AI access to our company data?

It can be when it's designed that way. We give AI only the access it needs for the task, limit agents to approved information sources, and in some cases remove AI from the process once the automation is built. We pair that with an AI policy, user training, and Data Loss Prevention controls.

Can defense contractors use AI automation?

Possibly, with the right guardrails. As a CMMC Registered Practitioner Organization, Workplace IT designs AI use around what data it can and can't touch so automation doesn't create compliance problems.

What does a typical first automation project look like?

Usually one well-defined task, such as an integration between two applications or automated user onboarding. Starting small proves the value quickly and shows where the next automation should go.
